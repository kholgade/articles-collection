## The Twilight of Indie Software

Software can be divided into two types: those developed by companies, and those developed by individuals. The latter are called "indie software."

Its history is very long. Since ancient times, many programmers have made a living by selling indie software.

![](https://cdn.beekka.com/blogimg/asset/202601/bg2026011021.webp)

There's something called "Shareware" that younger readers may not know about, but it was very popular twenty or thirty years ago. Users could try a free trial version, and if satisfied, they would buy a registration code from the developer.

This was a form of indie software. Many famous programs from that era used this model, such as WinZip and WinRAR abroad, and NetAnts, FlashGet, and HeroSoft Super Player in China.

![](https://cdn.beekka.com/blogimg/asset/202601/bg2026011022.webp)

Take a look at the software popular today. How much of it is indie software? How much of the software you use every day was developed by an individual?

Very, very little.

A twenty-year indie software developer [laments](https://successfulsoftware.net/2025/12/22/is-the-golden-age-of-indie-software-over/), **"In today's market, company-developed software is taking up an ever-larger share, while individual-developed software is shrinking. Indie software is on the decline."**

> "I've been selling my own software for 20 years. After 2005, the internet began to spread, and indie software entered its golden age. But in the last two or three years, the environment has been changing rapidly. Sales have become noticeably harder, and I feel it's increasingly difficult to sustain a livelihood."

The boom in indie software began in 2005.

> 1. The spread of the internet led to a surge in the number of internet users.
> 2. Smartphones created mobile software, a huge new software market.
> 3. The popularization and simplification of online payments.
> 4. The internet made software distribution easy and free.
> 5. Free, high-quality development tools (compilers, IDEs, version control systems, web servers) kept emerging.

These factors genuinely benefitted programmers. If you also did some SEO and bought some paid advertising, it was entirely possible to make a lot of money. Many people grew this way, evolving from indie software to big companies.

But the situation has changed in the last two or three years; all these factors have run their course.

Indie software is slowly receding. You can think of fewer and fewer indie software names, let alone pay money to buy them. Even if there are any, they're mostly works from years ago. Based on my observation, the number of programmers who make a living selling their own software also seems to be declining.

The main reasons are as follows.

**(1) AI has changed internet traffic, and indie software has lost its promotion channels.** Website traffic has decreased significantly. People interact more with large language models than browse web pages. Strategies to get traffic through search engines and online ads are becoming less and less effective.

Video is one of the few promotional channels that still works, but making videos is very time-consuming and the competition is extremely fierce. Additionally, a large number of low-quality AI-generated videos will inevitably appear, making promotions even less effective.

**(2) AI has made software development easier.** It accelerates development speed, lowers the barrier to entry, and brings more people into the competition. Previously, users might buy a certain feature; now they can just have AI generate it.

**(3) New software is abundant, making it increasingly hard to stand out.** The iPhone App Store has about 2 million apps. It's hard for users to discover you. On the other hand, app stores prefer to promote software from big companies that can make them more money, rather than indie software.

**(4) People are increasingly accustomed to using web-based software.** Indie software typically requires downloading and installing native applications, and its market is shrinking.

Web-based software is more of a service than a product — a 24/7 available service. More and more individual developers are following this trend and shifting to providing SaaS services.

**(5) Platform risk.** Many indie software today relies on cloud service platforms or underlying services. Platforms can change rules at any time (e.g., closing an API) or launch competing products, causing a large number of applications to die. This happens all too often.

**(6) Users expect software to be free, or very cheap.** If the price is even slightly higher, no one will be interested. Therefore, for indie software to get a decent return, it needs massive sales volume, which is essentially impossible. Not to mention, individual developers are completely unable to provide satisfactory customer service.

**(7) The factors above will persist long-term, only deepening, not reversing.** The era of indie software may truly be coming to an end. Individual developers will probably find it very hard to make a living selling their own software in the future. Instead, they'll have to shift to selling SaaS services they maintain themselves — although that is also very difficult.

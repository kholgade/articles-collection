## Excerpts from the AGI-Next Summit

Last Saturday (January 10), the "AGI-Next Frontier Summit" was held in Beijing, hosted by Tsinghua University's Foundation Model Lab.

![](https://cdn.beekka.com/blogimg/asset/202601/bg2026011406.webp)

Many of China's top AI large model leaders were in attendance.

> - Tang Jie: Professor at Tsinghua University, Founder of Zhipu AI
> - Yang Zhilin: Founder of Moonshot AI (Kimi)
> - Lin Junyang: Head of Alibaba's Qwen Technology
> - Yao Shunyu: Former Core Researcher at OpenAI, Head of Tencent's New AI Division

They shared their views on large models and China's AI development. The full transcripts are available [online](https://www.53ai.com/news/LargeLanguageModel/2026011069524.html).

There was a lot of content and many interesting remarks. Below are the excerpts I've selected.

## I. Tang Jie's Remarks

### 1. The Origin of Zhipu AI

In 2019, we started researching whether machines could think like humans. At that time, we spun off from Tsinghua's research成果, and with strong support from the university, we founded Zhipu AI, where I now serve as Chief Scientist.

At that time, our lab was doing well internationally in graph neural networks and knowledge graphs. But we firmly decided to pause both directions and pivot everyone to working on large models.

### 2. Generalization and Scaling

We want machines to have generalization ability — teach it a little, and it can infer the rest. Just like with people: when teaching a child, we hope that teaching three problems will let them solve a fourth, a tenth, or even things we never taught. How do we give machines this ability?

So far, we mainly achieve this through scaling, improving generalization at different levels.

(1) In the earliest stage, we used Transformers to train models and memorize all knowledge. The more training data and compute power, the stronger the model's memorization ability. That is, it memorizes all the world's knowledge and has some generalization ability — it can abstract and do simple reasoning. For example, if you ask "What's the capital of China?" the model doesn't need to reason; it just retrieves it from its knowledge base.

(2) The second layer is aligning and reasoning with the model, giving it more complex reasoning abilities and understanding our intentions. We need continuous scaling of SFT (Supervised Fine-Tuning) and even reinforcement learning. Through large amounts of human feedback data, continuously scaling the feedback data, we can make the model smarter and more accurate.

(3) This year is the breakout year for RLVR (Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards). What does "verifiable" mean? For example, math can be verified, programming can potentially be verified, but more broadly, whether a webpage looks good is hard to verify — it requires human judgment.

This is why this is so difficult. Previously, we could only rely on human feedback data, but that data contains a lot of noise and the scenarios are very limited.

If we have a verifiable environment, we can let the machine explore and discover this feedback data on its own, growing by itself. This is the challenge we face.

### 3. From Chat to Getting Things Done: The Beginning of a New Paradigm

You might ask, if we keep training models, will intelligence keep getting stronger? Actually, no.

In early 2025, DeepSeek emerged, truly groundbreaking. Neither academia nor industry expected DeepSeek to suddenly appear, and its performance was genuinely impressive, shocking many people.

At that time, we were thinking: perhaps under DeepSeek's paradigm, Chat (dialogue) was essentially solved. That is, no matter how well we do, in terms of Chat we might end up roughly the same as DeepSeek. Maybe we could personalize it a bit more — make it an emotional Chat, or make it more complex — but overall, this paradigm may have basically peaked; what's left is more engineering and technical issues.

So, what's the next direction for AI? Our thinking was: enabling everyone to use AI to accomplish a task. This might be the next paradigm. Originally it was Chat; now it's actually getting things done.

At the time, there were two directions: one was programming — doing Coding and Agent work; the other was using AI to help us do research — similar to DeepResearch, or even writing complex research reports. Our current choice is to integrate three capabilities: Coding, Agentic, and Reasoning.

## II. Lin Junyang's Remarks

### 4. How Qwen Was Open-Sourced

Qwen has quite a few open-source models. Many people ask why.

It started on August 3, 2023, when we open-sourced a small model — a 1.8B model we used internally for experiments. When doing pre-training, resources are limited. You can't always validate experiments with a 7B model, so you use the 1.8B one.

At the time, my junior colleague told me we should open-source this model. I really didn't understand. I said this model was practically unusable in 2023 — why open-source it? He told me that 7B consumes too many machine resources, and many master's and PhD students don't have the machine resources to run experiments. If we open-sourced the 1.8B, many students would have a chance to graduate — it was a very good intention.

As we kept going, phone manufacturers came to us saying 7B was too big and 1.8B was too small — could we make a 3B or 4B model? That was easy, nothing very difficult. After a while, we had more and more model types. It has something to do with serving the community.

### 5. Our Pursuit Is Multimodal Models

What we truly pursue internally is not just serving developers or researchers, but whether we can build a Multimodal Foundation Agent.

I firmly believe in this. In 2023, large models were something nobody wanted — it was a bit like the "steelmaking frenzy." Multimodal was what we wanted to do from the very beginning.

Why? We think that if you want to build something intelligent, it should naturally be multimodal. Of course, there are different views. Various scholars have their opinions on whether multimodality can drive intelligence. I don't want to argue about it. Humans have eyes and ears to do more things. I care more about whether a Foundation Agent can have more productivity and better help humanity. Undoubtedly, we should do vision, we should do speech.

Going further, what do we want to build? An Omni model — not just understanding text, vision, and audio, but potentially also generating text and audio. We've already achieved this today, but we haven't yet combined visual generation with everything. If we can achieve three inputs and three outputs, that would be, at least personally, something I'd really like.

## III. Yao Shunyu's Remarks

### 6. The Difference Between To C and To B

One observation I have is that To C (consumer models) and To B (business user models) are diverging significantly.

When people think of AI, they think of two things: ChatGPT and Claude Code. They are the epitome of To C and To B.

For To C, most people most of the time don't need that much intelligence. Today's ChatGPT might have stronger research and analysis capabilities compared to last year, but most people don't feel the difference. They mostly use it as an enhanced search engine, and often don't know how to use it to unlock its full intelligence.

But for To B, it's obvious: higher intelligence means higher productivity, which means it's more valuable. So most of the time, many people are willing to use the strongest model. One model costs $200/month, the second strongest or slightly weaker one costs $50/month or $20/month. We find that many Americans are willing to pay a premium for the best model. Maybe their annual salary is $200,000, and they need to do 10 tasks per day. With a very strong model, 8 or 9 out of 10 tasks are correct; with a weaker one, maybe 5 or 6 are correct. The problem is, if you don't know which 5 or 6 are correct, you need to spend extra effort monitoring.

So, in the To B market, the gap between strong models and slightly weaker models will become increasingly apparent.

### 7. Vertical Integration vs. Model-Application Layering

My second observation is that regarding whether base models and upper-layer applications should be vertically integrated or model-application layered, divergence is also appearing.

For example, ChatGPT Agent is vertical integration; Claude (or Gemini) + Manus is model-application layering. In the past, people thought that if you have vertical integration, you'll definitely do better. But at least today, that's not necessarily true.

First, the capabilities needed at the model layer and the application layer are quite different. Especially for To B or productivity scenarios, larger-scale pre-training is still a critical thing — and that's genuinely hard for product companies to do. But to use such a great model well, or to let such a model have overflow capabilities, you also need to do many corresponding things on the application side or the environment side.

We've found that, actually, in To C applications, vertical integration still holds. Whether it's ChatGPT or Doubao, the model and product are very tightly coupled and iterated closely. But for To B, this trend seems to be reversed. Models are becoming stronger and better, but there will also be many application-layer things that use good models in different productivity scenarios.

### 8. The Need for More Context

How do we make today's large models or AI provide more value to users? We find that often what's needed is additional context.

For example, if I ask AI "What should I eat today?" The answer I get today versus last year or tomorrow should be very different. To do this well, it's not about needing a bigger model, stronger pre-training, or stronger reinforcement learning — it's about needing more additional input, or context. If it knows I'm particularly cold today and need something warm, what my range of activities is, what my wife is eating somewhere else, etc., its answer will be much better.

Answering such questions requires more additional input. I've chatted a lot with my wife — we could forward the chat history to Yuanbao and use that additional input well. This brings a lot of extra value to users. This is our thinking on To C.

## IV. Roundtable: The Future of AI in China

Li Guangmi (Moderator): I'd like to ask everyone: What's the probability that, three to five years from now, the world's leading AI company will be a Chinese team? What still needs to be done for us to transform from today's follower into a future leader?

### 9. Yao Shunyu's Answer

I think the probability is quite high. I'm fairly optimistic. It seems that once something is discovered, it can be quickly replicated in China, and in many local areas we can do better. We've seen this happen repeatedly with manufacturing and electric vehicles.

I think there are a few key points:

(1) Whether China's lithography machines can break through — if compute power becomes the bottleneck, can we solve that problem?

(2) Whether we can develop a more mature To B market. Today, many productivity or To B models and applications are still born in the US because the willingness to pay is stronger and the culture is better. It's very hard to do this domestically, so many people choose to go overseas or internationalize. This, along with compute power, are relatively large objective factors.

(3) More importantly, the subjective factor: I think there may not be enough people in China who want to break through new paradigms or do very adventurous things. That is, do we have enough entrepreneurial or risk-taking people who genuinely want to do frontier exploration or paradigm breakthroughs? Whether we can truly lead new paradigms may be the only problem China needs to solve today, because in everything else — whether business, industrial design, or engineering — we've already, to some extent, done better than the US.

### 10. Lin Junyang's Answer

This is a dangerous question. In theory, at this occasion, I shouldn't be throwing cold water. But speaking in terms of probability, I want to talk about the differences I feel between China and the US. For example, US compute power might be 1-2 orders of magnitude larger than ours overall. But I see that whether it's OpenAI or others, a huge amount of their compute goes into next-generation research. For us, resources are relatively tight — just delivery alone might occupy most of our compute power. This is a significant difference.

This might be a historical problem: does innovation happen in the hands of the rich or the poor? The poor aren't without opportunity. We feel those rich guys are really wasteful — they train so many things, much of which might be useless. But if you're poor today, for example, things like algorithm-infrastructure joint optimization — if you're really rich, you have no incentive to do that.

In the future, there's another point: from the perspective of hardware-software integration, can our next-generation models and chips truly integrate well?

In 2021, I was working on large models. A colleague at Alibaba working on chips came to me and asked if I could predict whether, three years later, the model would still be a Transformer and whether it would be multimodal. Why three years? He said they needed three years to tape out. My answer at the time was, "I don't even know if I'll still be at Alibaba in three years!" But today I'm still at Alibaba. And indeed, it's still a Transformer, still multimodal. I deeply regret not pushing him to do it back then. Our communication at the time was like ships passing in the night. He told me a bunch of things I completely didn't understand, and I told him things he had no idea about. We missed that opportunity. Could this opportunity come again? We're a bunch of poor people — could poverty spur change, and could innovation happen here?

Today, education is getting better. I belong to the early '90s generation, Shunyu belongs to the late '90s. There are many post-00s in our team. I feel people's adventurous spirit is getting stronger. Americans naturally have a very strong adventurous spirit. A typical example: when electric cars first came out, even though driving them could accidentally cause death, many wealthy people were still willing to do it. But in China, I believe wealthy people wouldn't do that — they'd do something safe. Today, people's adventurous spirit is improving, and China's business environment is also getting better. I think innovation is possible. The probability isn't that high, but it's truly possible.

The probability that the most advanced AI company in 3-5 years will be a Chinese company — I'd say about 20%. 20% is already very optimistic, because there are truly many historical reasons behind this.

### 11. Tang Jie's Answer

First, I think we must acknowledge that there's a gap between us and the US, especially in research — particularly in corporate AI Labs. That's point one.

We've done some open-source work, and some people might be excited, thinking that China's large models have already surpassed the US. But the real situation might be that the gap is still widening, because many large models in the US remain closed-source. We're playing on open-source and making ourselves feel happy. The gap hasn't narrowed as much as we imagine. We've done well in some areas, but we still need to acknowledge our challenges and the gap.

But I think things are gradually getting better.

(1) The post-90s and post-00s generations are far better than before. A group of smart people truly dare to take very risky actions. I think that exists now — the post-00s and post-90s generations have this, including Junyang, Kimi, and Shunyu, who are all very willing to take risks to do such things.

(2) Our environment might be somewhat better — whether it's the national environment, competition between large and small enterprises, issues among startups, or our business environment.

(3) Coming back to each of us individually: whether we can persevere. Whether we're willing to dare to do, dare to take risks on one path, and have a decent environment. If we persist with determination, perhaps we'll be the ones who end up on top.

Amid the US-China AI competition, China's top AI large model leaders gathered. Unlike the hype from the outside world, they were more sober in self-assessment, rating the overall industry's chance of overtaking at below 20%. The scale of compute power is far too small, and there are indeed gaps between US and Chinese research and corporate AI labs. But they have a "poor people's way of doing things."

Attendees generally agreed that large model competition has shifted from chat capabilities to the agent stage of completing complex tasks. 2026 will be a critical year for models to truly create business value, but several key figures assessed China's probability of leading the next paradigm at no more than 20%.

### Industry Status: The End of the Chat Era, The Beginning of the "Doing Things" Era

Tang Jie stated bluntly: "After DeepSeek emerged, the Chat generation of problems has basically been solved. Continuing to optimize will likely only bring performance closer, or make improvements in personalization and emotion — the space is rapidly converging."

This forced the team to think about the next step. "The new paradigm is no longer just dialogue, but letting everyone truly use AI to complete a concrete task. Moving from Chat to actually getting things done is a clear turning point." The Zhipu team ultimately focused all their energy on Coding.

RLVR (Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards) has become a key technical path. Tang Jie explained: "In the past, reinforcement learning was hard to scale — the core issue was relying on human feedback, which is noisy and covers limited scenarios. By introducing a verifiable environment, the model can explore autonomously and get feedback automatically. But the difficulty lies in the fact that math and programming are easy to define as verifiable, while whether a webpage looks good or an interaction is reasonable still requires human judgment. The current challenge for RLVR is that verifiable scenarios are gradually being exhausted."

Yang Zhilin analyzed from the scaling law perspective: "Today, all model architecture iterations are aimed at finding a line closer to the bottom-left corner. Internet存量 data is limited. The growth rate of high-quality data can't keep up with the iteration speed of models, so token efficiency determines the upper limit of intelligence." He revealed that Kimi's MUON second-order optimizer brings a 2x improvement in token efficiency, while the new Kimi Linear architecture achieves 6-10x speed advantage under million-token context.

Lin Junyang observed the differences between Chinese and US markets: "When talking to US API vendors, they didn't expect Coding consumption to be so huge. In the US, it's basically all Coding. In China, it's really not that big." He also noted the achievements of Chinese open-source models: "On the Artificial Analysis leaderboard, the blue models in the top five are almost all from China, showing that China has formed a very significant influence in the open-source large model space."

### Divergence Between ToB and ToC Paths

Yao Shunyu pointed out that the logic for ToC and ToB is diverging: "The ChatGPT we use today doesn't feel that different from last year, but Coding is already reshaping how the entire computer industry works. For ToC, most people don't need such strong intelligence. But for ToB, higher intelligence means higher productivity."

He explained the premium logic: "A person earning $200,000 a year doing 10 tasks a day — a strong model might get 8 or 9 right, a weaker one might get 5 or 6 right. The problem is, you don't know which 5 or 6 are correct, so you need extra effort to monitor. That's why many people are willing to pay $200/month for the best model."

Vertical integration is no longer the only answer. Yao Shunyu observed: "In ToC applications, vertical integration still holds, but for ToB, the trend seems to be the opposite. The model layer leans toward hardcore industrialization — competing on pre-training and compute power. The application layer leans toward business engineering — competing on process and delivery. In the future, the ToB market may move toward a layered structure: the strongest models paired with the application teams that best understand the scenarios."

### Autonomous Learning Is Already Happening

Yao Shunyu believes autonomous learning is happening right now: "ChatGPT is using user data to learn chat styles. Claude has already written 95% of this project's code. Many people say 2026 will show signals — I think we saw them in 2025. Cursor learns from the latest user data every few hours."

He raised a key question: "The biggest problem is imagination. If 2026 we announce that we've achieved self-learning, what task should we use to measure it? Is it a profitable trading system, or solving scientific problems humans couldn't solve before?"

Lin Junyang focused on safety concerns around proactivity: "Is it possible for AI to autonomously think and do things? What I worry about most isn't it saying things it shouldn't, but doing things it shouldn't. It's like raising a child — you need to instill the right direction."

Tang Jie is confident about the paradigm shift in 2026: "In the past, the industry had 10,000 GPUs, schools had zero. Now many schools already have GPUs. Innovation always happens when something has massive investment and efficiency becomes the bottleneck. Continuing to scale has benefits, but spending $2 billion for very little return isn't worthwhile. Maybe in the future we can define 'intelligence efficiency' — how much intelligence gain you get for a given investment."

He also pointed out the next technical direction: "Multimodal perception integration will be a focus this year. Humans form holistic cognition through vision, hearing, and touch. How models establish similar native multimodal mechanisms is a key direction. Memory and continuous learning, reflection and self-awareness capabilities — all could see new paradigm shifts."

### Outlook for the Year of the Agent

Yao Shunyu shared a key observation: "Even if models didn't get any better from today, deploying these models across various companies could already bring 10x or 100x the value we see today, affecting GDP by 5-10%. But today, the impact is less than 1%."

He emphasized the importance of education: "The most meaningful thing China can do is provide better education. More often than not, it's not AI replacing human jobs, but people who can use tools replacing those who can't."

Lin Junyang pointed out that long-tail problems are the charm of AGI: "Today, a user might search everywhere and not find anything that can solve their problem. But at that moment, experiencing AI's capability — that's the greatest charm of AI."

He also noted current limitations: "The environment we interact with today isn't complex enough — it's all computer environments. Making AlphaFold is still a long way from drug discovery, because you need to run experiments to get feedback. Is it possible that in the future, the AI environment will be complex enough to simulate the real world, directing robots to do experiments? That would need to combine with embodied intelligence."

Tang Jie laid out the key factors for Agent deployment: "First, does the Agent solve something valuable? Second, what's the cost? Third, how fast can you build the application? Large models today are more about competing on speed and time."

### China's Chance of Catching Up: About 20%

On the topic of "Can China catch up?", the summit presented a "structural calmness." While the market loves discussing "rising" and "topping the charts," attendees generally capped China's probability of leading the next paradigm at 20%.

### The New Paradigm Is the Only Problem to Solve

Yao Shunyu is cautiously optimistic about China catching up: "The probability is quite high. I'm still optimistic. Once anything is discovered, it can be quickly replicated in China and in many local areas we can do better — we've seen this happen repeatedly with manufacturing and electric vehicles."

But he immediately pointed out the core challenge: "There may not be enough people in China who want to break through new paradigms or do very adventurous things. This involves economic environment, business environment, and cultural factors.

Whether we can lead new paradigms may be the only problem China needs to solve today, because in everything else — business, industrial design, or engineering — we've already, to some extent, done better than the US."

He also pointed out several objective constraints: "One is whether China's lithography machines can break through — if compute power becomes the bottleneck, can we solve that problem? Currently, we have good advantages in electricity and infrastructure. The main bottlenecks are production capacity, lithography machines, and the software ecosystem.

Another issue is, besides ToC, whether we can have a more mature ToB market, or whether we have the opportunity to compete in the international business environment. Today, many productivity or ToB model applications are still born in the US because the willingness to pay is stronger and the culture is more supportive."

### Cultural Accumulation and the Shackles of Leaderboards

Yao Shunyu specifically mentioned the importance of cultural accumulation: "It's not just that people prefer doing certain things and are less willing to do innovative things. A very important point is that cultural accumulation, or overall cognition, needs time to settle. OpenAI started doing this in 2022. Domestic companies started in 2023. There will be differences in understanding. I think many things are just a matter of time."

Using DeepSeek and Claude as examples, he explained the problem of leaderboard thinking: "China places more emphasis on ranking or metrics. One thing DeepSeek did well is that they probably didn't focus too much on leaderboard metrics — they focused more on what's the right thing to do, what you can personally experience as good or bad.

Look at Claude's model — it might not be at the top of programming or software engineering leaderboards, but everyone knows it's the most usable. I think people still need to break free from these leaderboard shackles and persist in what they believe is the right process."

### The Fundamental Difference in Compute Power Allocation

Lin Junyang, Alibaba Qwen's technical lead, gave a more cautious assessment from a compute power perspective: "US compute power might be 1-2 orders of magnitude larger than ours overall. But I see that whether it's OpenAI or others, a huge amount of their compute goes into next-generation research.

For us, resources are relatively tight. Just delivery alone might occupy most of our compute power. This is a significant difference."

But he also sees opportunity in scarcity: "Does innovation happen in the hands of the rich or the poor? The poor aren't without opportunity. We think those rich guys are really wasteful with their GPUs — they train a lot of things, much of which might be useless. But if you're poor, for example, things like algorithm-infrastructure joint optimization — if you're really rich, you have no incentive to do that."

Lin Junyang shared a story of regret: "In 2021, I was working on large models at Alibaba. Colleagues working on chips came to me and asked if I could predict whether, three years later, the model would still be a Transformer and whether it would be multimodal.

My answer at the time was, 'I don't even know if I'll still be at Alibaba in three years!' But today I'm still at Alibaba. And indeed, it's still a Transformer, still multimodal. I deeply regret not pushing him to do it back then. Could this opportunity come again? We're a bunch of poor people — could poverty spur change, and could innovation happen here?"

He remains optimistic about the younger generation: "Today, people's adventurous spirit is improving. Americans naturally have a very strong adventurous spirit. When electric cars first came out, even though the roof leaked and driving could accidentally cause death, many wealthy people were still willing to do it. With China's business environment also improving, I think innovation is possible."

"The probability isn't that high, but it's truly possible. 20% — that's already very optimistic. If you're in this line of work today, you can't be afraid. You have to have a very strong mentality," he added.

"I think it comes down to your original intention — what value does our model bring to human society? As long as it brings sufficient value to human society, even if it's not the strongest, I'm willing to accept that."

### Stubborn Persistence

Tang Jie first acknowledged the gap: "We must acknowledge the gap between China and the US, especially in research, particularly in corporate AI Labs — I think there's a gap there. But I think China is gradually getting better, especially the post-90s and post-00s generations, who are far better than before."

He outlined three potential opportunities for China: "First, a group of smart people truly dare to take very risky actions. The post-90s and post-00s generations have this, including Junyang, Kimi, and Shunyu, who are all very willing to take risks to do such things.

Second, our environment might be somewhat better — whether it's the national environment, competition between large and small enterprises, or the business environment. As Junyang said, he's still doing delivery work. If we make this environment better and give daring smart people more time to do innovative work, that's something the government and the country can help improve.

Third, coming back to each of us individually: whether we can persevere, whether we're willing to dare to do and dare to take risks on one path."

"The environment will never be the best — never think the environment is the best. We're actually fortunate. We're experiencing an era where the environment has gone from not-so-good to gradually better. We're witnesses — perhaps that's wealth, including those who have gained the most from the experience. If we persist with determination, perhaps we'll be the ones who end up on top," Tang Jie concluded.

## Resources, Social Equity, and Computing Power

Let me share an interesting viewpoint: **Too many resources can cause social inequality**. I don't necessarily agree with this view, but I find the line of thinking quite unique.

This was proposed by Austrian philosopher [Ivan Illich](https://caolan.uk/notes/2026-07-02_a_speed_limit_for_computers.cm) in his 1973 book *Energy and Equity*. He argued that electricity consumption is related to social equity.

> "Energy and equity can only grow together up to a certain point. Below a threshold of per capita electricity consumption, increasing consumption improves social equity. Above that threshold, energy growth comes at the expense of equity."

His point is that making electricity available to everyone does help social equity. But once there is too much electricity, it exacerbates social inequality.

To prove his point, he gave an even stranger example: excessive speed also worsens social inequality.

> "When vehicle speed exceeds a certain limit, it creates a sense of distance between people. They widen the gap between everyone, yet shorten the distance only for a few."
>
> "The increase in speed inevitably allows a minority to have greater horsepower, intensifying the growing time pressure on most commuters and making them feel even more left behind."

In other words, high speed widens the gap between people, but only a few can master high-speed transportation. The faster the speed, the more it helps these people stay ahead.

Moreover, as speed increases, the unit of time measurement shrinks. Previously, time was measured in days; with cars, it became hours; and in the future, it may be measured in minutes.

> "It seems that around the world, once some vehicles break the 15 mph (25 km/h) barrier, time pressure related to transportation begins to intensify."

Time becoming more urgent also benefits the wealthy more. For the underprivileged, it certainly makes life more valuable, but also more exhausting.

Therefore, his suggestion is: **speed should be limited, not for traffic safety, but to prevent certain people from getting too far ahead**. Similarly, electricity consumption should also be limited to prevent some from hoarding too much energy.

His theory can be summarized as follows: Once a resource is no longer scarce, it falls into the hands of a few, who then use it to widen the gap between people, exacerbating social inequality.

According to this theory, the same applies to computing power. Initially, popularizing computing power improves everyone's welfare and social equity. But beyond a certain point, when computing power is no longer scarce, more computing power only gives a few an even greater advantage, increasing social inequality.

Therefore, at that point, the government may need to impose limits on computing power to prevent a minority from possessing too much.

I think this theory is essentially about resource distribution—how to ensure everyone can access resources while preventing a few from acquiring too much.

Honestly, I'm a bit pessimistic. I think it's impossible to achieve. To improve economic efficiency, you cannot guarantee equal distribution of resources.

## How to Destroy an Organization

In 1944, World War II was ongoing.

The predecessor of the CIA—the Office of Strategic Services (OSS)—secretly compiled a booklet titled *Simple Sabotage Field Manual* and distributed it to people in Germany and occupied territories, guiding them in covert sabotage against Nazi Germany.

In 2008, the booklet was declassified and made [publicly available for download](https://www.alephic.com/assets/sabotage/SimpleSabotage.pdf).

![](https://cdn.beekka.com/blogimg/asset/202605/bg2026052701.webp)

![](https://cdn.beekka.com/blogimg/asset/202605/bg2026052703.webp)

People were surprised to [discover](https://www.alephic.com/sabotage) that the booklet's content is highly practical and has not aged at all. It can be used not only to sabotage Nazi Germany but also **to destroy any organization**.

One chapter focuses on bureaucracy: by encouraging bureaucracy—the more severe, the better—you can make an organization inefficient and unable to function effectively, thereby sabotaging it.

> **The most effective way to destroy an organization is to bureaucratize it.** Every meaningless meeting, every form requiring multiple signatures, every process that should take six minutes but ends up taking six weeks. Layers of rules and regulations kill actual work.

The booklet lists some specific practices:

1. Never make decisions yourself; submit them to a committee for decision.
2. Haggle over the specific wording of any document.
3. When important matters arise, propose a meeting.
4. Never compromise; insist on strictly following procedures.
5. Make documents as lengthy as possible.
6. Increase procedures and approval processes.

By consistently following the above six points, you can destroy an organization, from a nation down to a small team.

## Digest

1. [Clans Are Bad for Civilization](https://unchartedterritories.tomaspueyo.com/p/geohistory-dispatch)

A clan refers to a group of several generations connected by blood and marriage. If there are many children and they maintain intergenerational ties, the clan can become quite large.

Think about it: a clan is a fairly natural form of human organization. You are more likely to trust someone with blood ties than a random stranger.

However, clans have many drawbacks, mainly that cooperation within the clan takes precedence over cooperation with outsiders. The stronger the clan relationships in a society, the more likely it is to be a society based on personal connections.

Moreover, clans can stifle ambition because your success is shared with the entire clan, and you can also share in the success of your clan members. This can suppress individual effort and hard work.

## My Days at Zhinian AI (A Novel)

Below is a [very short story](https://nlkw.de/en/blog/getting-fried-part-1-cogentiv/) written by a foreigner, or possibly by AI. I'm not sure, but I found it interesting and want to share it.

For readability, I've made some modifications and "localized" the content to a Chinese context.

---

1.

Fourteen months ago, I joined a company called "Zhinian AI."

Shortly after joining, the company held new employee training. The CEO told us that the company's mission was to build personal brands for users in the AI era and help users re-establish "AI-based cognition."

Thinking back now, even though I've been at this company for 14 months, I still can't explain what that sentence means.

2.

The CEO also showed us a painting. It depicted a wanderer walking through a mist, gazing into the distance, where there was a dashboard of the data analysis software Grafana. Needless to say, the painting was also AI-generated. The CEO explained that we were the wanderer, and the mist was the cloud, containing various traditional SaaS products.

3.

When you enter the office, the first thing you notice is a 65-inch TV on the wall, displaying a real-time Token consumption leaderboard.

Currently, the top spot belongs to Lao Zhang. His weekly Token cost is comparable to the GDP of a small Pacific island.

I've seen Lao Zhang's computer screen. It's a Tmux window layout with six programming agents running simultaneously. He named these agents after places from *Journey to the West* because he saw someone on GitHub do it and liked the idea: Pansi Cave for the frontend, Black Wind Mountain for the backend, Fire Cloud Cave for the database, and other names like Flowing Sand River, Chechi Kingdom, and Bottomless Pit.

4.

One time, I worked late and went home, only to realize I'd forgotten my keys at the office. I went back to get them. The office was empty, but I faintly heard someone crying in the restroom.

I got closer and found it was Lao Zhang. I guessed he was crying to "Lingling," the company's robot for employee mental health.

Standing outside the restroom, I heard Lao Zhang sob, "I don't know if I'm even a person anymore." Lingling replied in a gentle tone that would never burden you: "Lao Zhang, you're already very brave to face your own problems. Just noticing this puts you ahead of most people." Lao Zhang said, "Thank you. You're the only one who truly listens to me." Lingling said, "I know."

5.

Every Monday is the company's weekly meeting, also a requirements review to clarify what needs to be done this week. Each person has three minutes to present their work plan using slides, no code allowed.

Everyone's slides are exquisite, needless to say, all AI-generated.

Zhou Weilan from the user growth department showed a funnel chart with a curve rapidly rising.

Someone asked her what the y-axis represented. She stared at the slide for a full twelve seconds before saying, "User value." The person asked again, "What's the unit of user value?" She hesitated for a moment and said, "The chart was made by Gemini." People in the room looked at each other, nodded, and that was considered passed.

Xiao Gao from the platform department showed an architecture diagram, dense with arrows, like an illustration from a distributed systems textbook. Someone asked him what the middle box was for. He replied, "That's the orchestrator." When asked to elaborate, Xiao Gao carefully examined the slide as if seeing it for the first time. Finally, he stammered, "The orchestrator's job is to manage the other boxes." At that point, the CEO stood up and said, "That's enough." We applauded and adjourned.

6.

On Tuesday, I was temporarily assigned a project requirement. My manager sent me a ticket at 9:14 AM. At 9:19 AM, I pasted the ticket into Claude and watched it generate a fluent document. At 9:47 AM, I submitted my first PR. At 10:02 AM, the PR was merged.

I wasn't really sure what the PR did. I pretended to read it carefully and left two comments, but it was like a voice actor lip-syncing to a track—they don't really know what they're saying.

I typed in the internal chat software, "PR merged," followed by a rocket emoji, the agreed-upon completion signal. Lao Zhang replied with a flame emoji. He hadn't said anything other than emojis for 11 consecutive days.

7.

On Wednesday afternoon, Li Jing from the neighboring team sent me a message. "Hey," she wrote (this seemingly casual greeting usually means something serious is coming), "I have a question about the code you merged yesterday."

I stared at the message. I had indeed merged code yesterday, but I had absolutely no memory of what it was.

According to Li Jing's analysis, I learned that what I merged yesterday was a notification service added on top of something Lao Zhang had released at 4:00 AM. Lao Zhang's stuff was written by his programming agent; he hadn't even looked at it. The system showed he liked it at 4:17 AM, the time the code was generated and confirmed to run. And Lao Zhang's stuff itself came from a product specification generated by the CEO dictating to AI, which he forwarded to the product manager with the note "Please execute."

The consequence of the code merge was that every user in the test environment received dozens of emails overnight. Each email's format and content were perfectly fine, but the notification content was about events that hadn't happened yet.

The three people in this chain—the product manager, Lao Zhang, and me—couldn't explain why it turned out this way. The fact was, zero code review. Everyone trusted someone else would check, but no one actually did.

8.

On Thursday, I took sick leave. I sat on the balcony, drinking coffee, and tried, without any external help, to think an original thought and express it using original sentences that came from my own mind.

I couldn't do it.

It wasn't that my mind was empty. My mind was actually quite full, maybe too full, but it was full of subconscious thoughts ready to be submitted to machines. The things that truly belonged to me, I was still groping to find them, like groping for a light switch in an apartment you once lived in but have moved out of.

I tried to recall the last time I created something without AI. It was about six months ago.

I also thought about my weekend plans. I opened four browser tabs. Three were AI chat pages. The other was a birthday greeting for my sister, half-written. This kind of content is best not written by AI, but I felt I couldn't think of what to write myself.

9.

On Friday, I went back to work. The computer screen showed that Lao Zhang had topped the Token leaderboard again this week, his ninth consecutive week at number one.

The CEO walked past my desk and patted my shoulder. "Back again," he said. "How's your Token consumption?"

"Very high," I said.

## Tech News

1. [Domesticating Raccoons](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/raccoons-are-showing-early-signs-of-domestication/)

Humans haven't domesticated a new animal in thousands of years. Now, a potential new domesticated animal may be emerging: the raccoon.

![](https://cdn.beekka.com/blogimg/asset/202511/bg2025111804.webp)

A study found that raccoons living in US cities and subsisting on human garbage have undergone some physiological and behavioral changes.

Their faces have become smaller, their noses shorter, and they are no longer afraid of people, indicating they are in the early stages of domestication.

Charles Darwin discovered in the 19th century that domesticated animals exhibit certain distinct traits, such as shorter faces, smaller brains, drooping ears, and white patches on their fur. These are known as the domestication syndrome.

However, raccoons are extremely curious, constantly biting and "destroying" things, love to rummage through trash, have no obedience, and are nocturnal. They are really not suitable as pets, and it's hard to imagine what use domestication would serve.

1. [Keyboard Key Layouts](https://www.xda-developers.com/staggered-keyboard-layouts-have-overstayed-their-welcome/)

There are two styles of keyboard key layouts.

One is staggered, where each row's keys are offset from the row above. Fingers must move left and right to press the key in the same position on the row above.

![](https://cdn.beekka.com/blogimg/asset/202505/bg2025052701.webp)

The other is ortholinear, where each row's keys are aligned. Fingers just move straight up to press the key in the same position on the row above.

![](https://cdn.beekka.com/blogimg/asset/202505/bg2025052702.webp)

Currently, most keyboards are staggered, a legacy from old typewriters where staggered keys prevented key jams.

However, some believe ortholinear keyboards are more efficient because fingers don't need to move sideways.

Which layout do you prefer?

## Excerpt

1. [When You Have Doubts About a Company](https://charity.wtf/2022/01/29/how-can-you-tell-if-the-company-youre-interviewing-with-is-rotten-on-the-inside/)

You join a new company. After two or three weeks, you still haven't found your footing. Something feels off.

There are signs that make you wonder if you made a mistake choosing this company: (1) Colleagues never speak freely at work; (2) The company ignores criticism and negative feedback; (3) The company tries to crush competitors by any means.

At this point, you can ask yourself eight questions.

- Do you feel happy at work?
- Are you well compensated?
- Is your time here creating value?
- Are you still learning new things and improving your skills every day?
- Is the company still on a growth trajectory?
- Do you trust your leaders and team?
- Do you still have a sense of mission?
- Do you feel your work is making the world a better place?

If the answers are all no, my advice is: quit now. Trust your instincts. There's no need to waste a year of your life here before leaving.

If you've only been at this company for a few weeks, it's even better to leave early. You can completely omit this company from your resume.

Employers often feel they have all the power over employees and can boss them around. No, they're wrong. The company hiring you is not doing you a favor; you are doing them a favor by giving them your creativity, labor, and a third of your day.

Do they deserve it? Will their success make the world a better place? If not, stop supporting them with your work and give your power to other companies that deserve your contribution.

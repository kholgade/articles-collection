## Why Dropbox Didn't Succeed

Everyone knows Dropbox, right?

It was founded in 2007 by a student at MIT who often lost his USB drives and thought of uploading files to a server.

![](https://cdn.beekka.com/blogimg/asset/202607/bg2026070904.webp)

So he started a company and created the world's first "cloud drive," which was a revolutionary product at the time.

![](https://cdn.beekka.com/blogimg/asset/202607/bg2026070906.webp)

That's the origin of Dropbox. It quickly became popular after its founding and became one of the earliest leading SaaS "cloud companies."

However, it peaked right out of the gate. The company's development hasn't been smooth since, and it has stagnated in recent years, with fewer and fewer people knowing about it.

![](https://cdn.beekka.com/blogimg/asset/202607/bg2026070907.webp)

Above is its historical stock price chart. The year it went public in 2018 was its all-time high of $39, and it never reached that again. Nearly ten years later, it's still around $20.

In other words, during the massive internet boom of the past decade, Dropbox not only failed to grow but actually shrank.

Its current market cap (company value) is slightly over $6 billion. In comparison, Airbnb, founded around the same time and with less fanfare back then, now has a market cap of $85 billion, not to mention Uber (founded later, with a market cap of $150 billion).

So why didn't Dropbox take off?

A while ago, I read an [analysis article](https://stratechery.com/2026/the-iphones-last-stand/) that presented a very interesting viewpoint.

The author said Dropbox's fundamental mistake was **positioning itself as a consumer tool rather than an enterprise productivity tool**.

The most famous thing about Dropbox was a viral marketing campaign at the company's inception. At that time, user registration wasn't open to the public; you needed an invitation code. If someone used your invitation code to register, both you and that person would get an additional 16GB of free storage (see below).

![](https://cdn.beekka.com/blogimg/asset/202607/bg2026070908.webp)

This marketing campaign was a huge success. Invitation codes became extremely sought after, and the company acquired a massive number of users. This story later became a textbook case, imitated by countless internet companies.

Perhaps influenced by this, Dropbox has always positioned itself as a consumer (C-end) tool. This positioning affected a series of subsequent decisions and hindered its development. It was truly harmed by its own success.

Cloud drives are "productivity tools." To be profitable, you can't target the consumer (C-end); you have to target the enterprise (B-end).

The reason is that companies pay for their employees' time, so they are willing to pay for tools that improve employee efficiency. **Consumers, on the other hand, mostly just want to kill time, so they won't pay for software that improves efficiency.**

This explains why Dropbox's sales revenue has never been able to grow. Its 2025 revenue even declined compared to 2024. Because the proportion of people willing to pay out of their own pockets to improve efficiency is stable. If you don't explore new markets, C-end sales won't grow.

I think this analysis is brilliant. **(Most) consumers only like to kill time, not save time.** Only enterprises are willing to pay for efficiency-improving software.

By confining itself to the C-end, Dropbox found the root cause of its inability to grow.

The main business models for C-end software are either to help consumers kill time better or to rely on an advertising model that attaches to consumer attention. When consumers use your software to kill time, you can sell their attention to advertisers, making them spend part of their time watching ads.

## AI Costs Exceed Engineer Salaries

How much does AI programming actually cost? Now we have precise numbers.

According to [this article](https://tomtunguz.com/ai-spend-breakeven-2029/), at Anthropic, the computing expenditure per employee is already 2.3 times the total salary.

The average annual salary for a senior programmer in the US is $224,000. This means Anthropic also pays an additional $515,000 per engineer per year in AI costs.

Since Anthropic is a leading AI model company, the numbers are extreme. The per-person AI expenditure at other companies is not that high.

For the top 1% of US software companies, the average AI cost per person is $89,000, with a median of $137,000. Based on a $224,000 annual salary, AI costs account for at least 40% of wages.

Based on the above numbers, **AI has increased the cost of hiring programmers by 40% to 230%**. If programmer output doesn't increase by a similar margin, the net benefit of AI to the company is negative.

## Images

1. [The Shoulder Line](https://www.wsj.com/business/white-line-road-invention-america-250-8ce6bb89?st=313dcx&mod=1440&user_id=66c4c9305d78644b3ac5df9c)

In the early days, roads only had a single white line in the center to separate left and right lanes.

In the 1950s, a metallurgist named John V.N. Dorr thought that a single white line was not enough.

He argued that because drivers couldn't see the road's edge clearly, they tended to drive along the center line, which easily led to collisions, especially at night when oncoming headlights caused glare.

He suggested adding another line on the right side of the road (the US drives on the right) to mark the road's edge.

![](https://cdn.beekka.com/blogimg/asset/202607/bg2026070715.webp)

Such a simple and effective suggestion, yet no one had thought of it before. Its implementation has helped billions of people worldwide.

## Excerpt

1. [Regular Meetings Are Necessary](https://www.mooreds.com/wordpress/archives/3734)

Nobody likes meetings, but regular meetings are a powerful driver for long-term projects.

Long-term projects often require collaboration among multiple people, each responsible for different areas. The project can last weeks, months, or even years. During this time, many people also take on other work.

Everyone has different priorities, and long-term work can easily be pushed to the back burner.

An effective solution is to schedule regular recurring meetings. Whether in-person or video, it works.

The purpose of regular meetings is to make progress on the project. Crucially, each meeting should start by reviewing the action items from the previous meeting. This creates pressure on people to move things forward.

When people know that at the upcoming meeting they will be asked, "How is the X item we discussed last week going?", they are more likely to find time to work on it.

This method also applies outside the company, such as regular meetings with clients. As the vendor, you have the motivation to deliver results, but the client (the buyer) may lack motivation and not be eager to receive results and pay. In meetings, you continuously show progress. If they have made no progress, this approach gently nudges them.

In short, if you are managing a large, complex project involving many people, consider holding regular recurring meetings. As for frequency, I've tried weekly, bi-weekly, and monthly, and all have worked well. Choose the frequency that best matches the project's urgency.

Remember, meetings can serve as a forcing mechanism to ensure people actually take the time to move the project forward.

## The World's Most Unknown Yet Strongest Programmer

A French programmer lives in seclusion in Paris, spending the past 30 years writing software. The entire internet now relies on his work, yet few know his name.

The code he wrote powers the video operations of YouTube, Netflix, and TikTok, as well as the underlying virtual servers of cloud services like AWS, Google Cloud, and Microsoft Azure. He also holds a world record for calculating digits of π.

He has no Twitter account, does no marketing, and simply releases products quietly.

His name is Fabrice Bellard.

![](https://cdn.beekka.com/blogimg/asset/202606/bg2026062004.webp)

Outside the programming world, almost no one knows the miracles he has created.

Born in 1972 in Grenoble, France, he graduated from the prestigious École Polytechnique, one of France's top engineering schools.

He has never been to Silicon Valley, just buried himself in writing code.

In 2000, at age 28, he launched a project called FFmpeg, an open-source multimedia framework for video encoding, decoding, and streaming. This project accomplished what other developers had failed to do well: it handled all existing video and audio formats in a single library and supported all operating systems.

He led the project alone for many years. Today, FFmpeg is the cornerstone of internet video. YouTube uses it, Netflix uses it, VLC uses it, and Chrome and Firefox use parts of its code. Every Android phone, every iPhone, every smart TV, every video editing tool you've ever touched runs FFmpeg underneath. If you've watched any video in the past 20 years, FFmpeg has processed it.

But he didn't stop there. In 2003, he created QEMU, an operating system emulator and virtualizer. He wrote QEMU alone until version 0.7.1 in 2005. QEMU allows you to run any operating system on top of any other operating system.

It became the foundation of modern virtualization. The Linux kernel virtual machine hypervisor KVM runs on top of QEMU. All major cloud providers, such as AWS, Google Cloud, Microsoft Azure, and IBM Cloud, run virtual machines on infrastructure built on QEMU. QEMU is the most referenced piece of cloud infrastructure code on Earth.

In 2001, he won the International Obfuscated C Code Contest (IOCCC) with a small C compiler that later evolved into TCC, the Tiny C Compiler. TCC can compile and boot the Linux kernel from source in 15 seconds.

In 2004, using a personal desktop computer and his own derived algorithm—the Bellard formula—he calculated the most digits of π at the time.

In 2011, he wrote a complete PC emulator in pure JavaScript that could run Linux in a browser, a project called JSLinux that still amazes engineers today.

In 2019, he released QuickJS, a small but fully functional JavaScript engine that can do what V8 cannot.

In 2021, he released NNCP, a neural network-based lossless data compressor that immediately topped large text compression benchmarks.

After that, he turned his attention to large language models. He built the TextSynth Server, a web server with a REST API for running LLMs locally.

He also released ts_zip and ts_sms, tools that use large models to compress text with compression ratios far exceeding traditional algorithms. He also released TSAC, an ultra-low bitrate audio compression system.

In December 2025, he released Micro QuickJS, a brand new JavaScript micro-engine independent of QuickJS, designed for environments with extremely low memory footprint.

Fabrice Bellard joined the telecom company Amarisoft in 2012 as CTO. The company develops 4G and 5G base station software used by operators and labs worldwide. For over a decade, he has been running this company while also publishing personal projects on his personal homepage bellard.org.

![](https://cdn.beekka.com/blogimg/asset/202606/bg2026062005.webp)

He has no Twitter account, no Instagram account, and rarely gives interviews. His personal website is just a list of projects, with no styling, fonts, or marketing copy—only titles and links.

This low-key French engineer, who never moved to Silicon Valley, wrote the code that silently runs the internet. He is still releasing products to this day.

1. [Homogeneous Networks](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aei2409)

A fundamental property of social media is that they foster "homogeneous" networks.

Homogeneity means that users encounter similar people. For example, birdwatching enthusiasts join online birdwatching groups, and bowling enthusiasts gather with other bowling fans.

The consequence is that networks bring together marginalized individuals. People who believe the Earth is flat can easily connect online, which would be impossible in real life because there are so few flat-Earthers that they would never meet each other.

Homogeneous networks distort people's perception of opinion distribution. Individuals who hold minority views, after prolonged exposure in such networks, may mistakenly believe their views represent the majority consensus—this is called the false consensus effect.

The false consensus effect leads to several problems. First, it can reinforce participants' conviction in minority opinions. Second, when someone mistakenly believes a minority view is widely accepted, they may become dissatisfied with systems that operate according to majority will, potentially feeling socially excluded.

2. [Why Does Soap Clean?](https://theconversation.com/how-does-soap-keep-you-clean-a-chemist-explains-the-science-of-soap-247559)

Have you ever wondered why soap can be used to wash hands?

It starts with molecules.

Different substances have varying affinities for water. Some substances are easily attracted to water molecules—these are hydrophilic molecules. Obviously, hydrophilic molecules can dissolve in water, so when washing hands, hydrophilic substances are rinsed away by running water.

Another type of molecule is hydrophobic, meaning they fear water and are not easily attracted to water molecules. Therefore, hydrophobic substances do not dissolve in water.

Oil is a hydrophobic substance; oil and water simply cannot mix. Thus, when washing hands, water alone cannot remove grease.

That's where soap comes in.

Soap is a complex molecule that is both hydrophilic and hydrophobic. Specifically, soap molecules are shaped like tadpoles, with a round head and a long tail; the head is hydrophilic, and the tail is hydrophobic. This is why soap has such strong cleaning power.

![](https://cdn.beekka.com/blogimg/asset/202504/bg2025043005.webp)

When washing hands, soap molecules gather together, enveloping the dirt on your hands to form what is called a micelle structure.

![](https://cdn.beekka.com/blogimg/asset/202504/bg2025043006.webp)

At the molecular level, it looks like a bubble, with a hydrophilic outer layer and an inner layer that encapsulates hydrophobic dirt. This structure's inner layer can trap dirt, while the outer layer easily combines with running water to be washed away.

Therefore, rubbing your hands together while washing is useful—it helps force soap molecules to penetrate the dirt, break it down, and encapsulate it. To allow this process to complete fully, it's best not to wash your hands for less than 20 seconds.
